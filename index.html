The Bezold effect

Change one colour in a pattern and the whole design shifts. With black lines the red field looks deeper; with white lines it looks lighter and pinker. This is assimilation, the opposite of contrast. Wilhelm von Bezold noticed it while designing rugs.

The illusion of transparency

Three opaque colours can look like two transparent sheets. It works only when the overlap colour sits between the two in hue and value. Slide it toward one colour and that sheet seems to lie on top.

Light adds, pigment subtracts

Overlapping coloured lights add up toward white (screens, stage lights). Overlapping inks and paints remove light and head toward black: cyan, magenta and yellow are the true subtractive primaries. Real paint does both at once, which is why the studio uses Kubelka–Munk physics instead of either rule.

How we see colour.

Colour doesn't live in objects. It's made by light, three kinds of cone cells and the brain's opponent channels, so it changes with dusk, context and who is looking.

Three cones and the rods

Short (S), medium (M) and long (L) wavelength cones overlap heavily; colour is the brain comparing their signals. Rods handle dim light and see no colour at all.

The eye then encodes three opponent channels: red against green, blue against yellow, and light against dark. That's why no colour looks reddish-green or yellowish-blue, and why complementary colours feel like true opposites.

Afterimages

Stare at the dot for 20 seconds. When the image disappears, the opposite colours appear: tired cones, opponent channels rebounding.

The Purkinje shift

As light fades, vision hands over from cones to rods. Reds darken first, and blues and greens look relatively brighter. Painters of night and dusk use this deliberately.

Colour constancy

Tiles A and B are exactly the same grey. Your brain "discounts" the shadow and sees B as a light tile in shade, the same way a white page still looks white under a warm lamp. Painters must paint the colour they see, not the colour they know.

Colour and the other senses

Research on cross-modal correspondences finds that people widely share certain associations, though culture and experience shape them.

Temperature

Reds and oranges read as warm, blues as cool. Warm colours tend to advance, cool ones recede: the basis of atmospheric depth in painting.

Weight and size

Dark, saturated colours look heavier; light colours feel lighter and larger. Designers place dark masses low to steady a composition.

Taste and smell

Studies link pink and red with sweetness, yellow-green with sourness and dark brown or black with bitterness. Packaging uses this deliberately.

Sound

Higher pitches pair with lighter, brighter colours, lower pitches with darker ones. Some people, called synaesthetes, involuntarily see colours with sounds. Explore it in Colour & sound.

Fix the usual problems.

The questions painters and students ask most, and what actually solves them.

My mixes keep turning muddy

Mud usually means too many pigments (more than three), or warm and cool versions fighting each other. Cap each pile at two or three paints, mix with a clean knife, and choose pigments biased toward the target: a cool yellow and a cool blue for clean greens. Remember that "mud" in one context is a beautiful grey in another: it's only wrong if it doesn't fit.

My tints look chalky

Titanium white cools and dulls colour. Lighten with a lighter neighbour first (yellow for orange, for example), add only as much white as needed, or use zinc white or a touch of the colour's warm neighbour to restore warmth.

Darks look dead and flat

Black straight from the tube kills chroma. Mix chromatic darks instead: ultramarine + burnt sienna, phthalo green + alizarin, or dioxazine violet + a yellow-orange. They read as black but stay alive.

Acrylics dried a different colour

Wet acrylic binder is milky, so the colour you mix looks lighter than it will dry. Mix slightly lighter than your target and test on a scrap. In the studio here, switch Wet/Dry to preview the change.

Watercolor dried too pale

Watercolor dries lighter and duller, often by 10–30%. Mix stronger than you think, use more pigment and less water for darks, and judge only when dry.

Cauliflowers / blooms in washes

Water flowed back into a drying wash. Keep the water on your brush drier than the wash on the paper, or let it dry fully. Embrace blooms on purpose for skies and foliage.

Greens look artificial

Tube greens (especially phthalo) are more intense than most foliage. Mix your own from yellow + blue, warm them with an earth or red, and vary them constantly across the painting.

Skin tones look grey or orange

Start with an earth triad: yellow ochre, a red (vermilion or cadmium red light), and white, plus a cool (ultramarine or black) to neutralise. Warm the lights, cool the half-tones, and watch the temperature, not just the value.

Oil painting is cracking

Usually lean-over-fat: a fast-drying lean layer was painted over a slower, oilier one. Follow fat over lean, avoid excessive solvent, and let thick layers cure.

Dull patches in my oil painting

"Sinking in": oil was absorbed by the layer beneath. Oil out with a thin film of medium wiped back before continuing, or varnish when the painting is fully cured.

Whites are turning yellow

Linseed oil yellows, especially in the dark. Use whites ground in safflower or poppy oil for the lightest passages, and hang paintings in light.

Pastel won't stick anymore

The tooth is full. Brush off excess, use a light spray of workable fixative to restore grip, or work on sanded paper, which takes many more layers.

Paper buckles under water

Use 300 gsm (140 lb) or heavier paper, stretch it, or tape it to a board. Blocks (glued on four sides) stay flat.

Paint peeling off

Adhesion failure: acrylic over oil, painting on a greasy surface, or binder over-diluted with water on a non-absorbent ground. Use medium rather than water for very thin layers and paint on a properly primed surface.

Colours look different in daylight

Light source matters: warm bulbs push everything yellow. Paint under neutral daylight-balanced lighting (about 5000–5500 K, high CRI) and check work in natural light.

My painting has no sense of light

Light comes from contrast in value and temperature, not white paint. Group your values (squint), keep shadows and lights clearly separated, and choose a light temperature: warm light means cool shadows, and vice versa.

A colour is too strong and takes over

High-tinting-strength pigments (phthalos, dioxazine, Prussian) dominate. Add them to the lighter colour, never the other way around, and in tiny amounts.

Can't lift colour out of my watercolor

Staining pigments (phthalos, quinacridones, dioxazine) bind to the paper fibres. Use non-staining pigments (cobalt, ultramarine, earths) where you plan to lift.

Everything looks the same intensity

Give the eye a hierarchy: keep most of the painting at moderate saturation and reserve your highest chroma for the focal area. Bright colours sing next to greys.

My mix matches the photo but looks wrong

Photos crush shadows and shift colour. Squint at the subject, compare colours to their neighbours rather than in isolation, and adjust for the surrounding colours (simultaneous contrast).

Colours fade over time

Choose ASTM lightfastness I or II paints. Fugitive pigments (genuine alizarin, some dye inks, cheap pinks) fade in sunlight. Check the pigment code on the tube.

Which paints are toxic?

Cadmium, cobalt, lead (flake white), chromium and manganese pigments need care: don't eat or drink while painting, don't sand or spray them, and wash hands. Pastel dust should not be blown off; tap it into a damp cloth.

Frequently asked

Why do blue and yellow make green in paint but grey on screen?

Screens add light; paint subtracts it. A blue pigment absorbs red and yellow light, a yellow pigment absorbs blue and violet, and the one band both reflect is green. Chromaraga's mixer models this with Kubelka–Munk absorption and scattering at 36 wavelengths instead of averaging RGB values.

How much water can I add to acrylic paint?

On primed, non-absorbent surfaces Golden reports its Heavy Body and Fluid acrylics stay durable when thinned around 1:1 with water; on absorbent surfaces fluid acrylics can take much more. For very thin glazes, use acrylic medium instead of water.

What does fat over lean mean in oil painting?

Each layer should contain as much or more oil than the layer beneath it. Lean, solvent-rich layers go first; oil-rich layers go last, so lower layers dry first and the paint film doesn't crack.

Why does my watercolor dry lighter?

Wet paint is glossy and saturated; as the water evaporates the surface turns matte and scatters more light. Expect watercolor to dry noticeably lighter and mix darker than you need.

How do I mix a colour from a photo?

Open the Studio, upload your photo under "Match a colour", tap the spot you want, choose your medium and paints, and Chromaraga calculates the recipe in parts, percentages and millilitres.

What are the three properties of colour?

Hue (the colour family), value (light or dark) and chroma or saturation (intensity). Value carries form and readability more than any other property.

What is a split primary palette?

A warm and a cool version of each primary (for example lemon and cadmium yellow, cadmium red and quinacridone magenta, ultramarine and phthalo blue) plus white. It mixes clean colours all around the wheel.

Lessons for students and teachers.

A progression from first value scale to advanced colour interaction. Each lesson opens the right tools, ready to use in a classroom or on your own.

Lesson 1, beginner

Paint a nine-step value scale

Mix black and white into nine even steps from 1 (near black) to 9 (near white). Value carries form more than hue does.

Lesson 2, beginner

Build a split-primary wheel

With a warm and a cool version of each primary, mix all twelve hues. Notice which pairs make clean versus dull secondaries.

Lesson 3, beginner

Tints, tones and shades

Take one hue and make it lighter with white, greyer with grey, darker with black. Compare with darkening by a complement.

Lesson 4, intermediate

Neutralise without black

Make a row of greys and browns by mixing complements: ultramarine + burnt sienna, red + green, violet + yellow.

Lesson 5, intermediate

The Zorn palette

Anders Zorn is said to have painted with just yellow ochre, vermilion, ivory black and white. Paint a portrait or still life with only these four.

Lesson 6, intermediate

Warm light, cool shadow

Paint a simple object in golden-hour light. Keep lights warm and shadows cool and violet; never just "darker".

Lesson 7, intermediate

Match five colours from a photo

Upload a reference, pick five key colours, and mix each one physically. Compare your swatch to the screen.

Lesson 8, advanced

Albers: one colour as two

From Interaction of Color: make a single colour look like two different colours by changing only its surroundings.

Lesson 9, advanced

Glaze versus scumble

Lay a transparent colour over a dry light layer (glaze), then an opaque light veil over a dark (scumble). Compare depth and temperature.

Lesson 10, advanced

Notan and value grouping

Simplify a reference into 2, 3 then 5 values before choosing any colour. Many strong paintings work in three values.

Lesson 11, advanced

Atmospheric perspective

Paint five layers of hills, each lighter, bluer and lower in contrast toward the horizon.

Lesson 12, teacher

Make a mixing chart

Every pigment mixed 1:1 with every other. The studio can generate the chart for any palette so students can then paint the real one.

Hindustani ragas belong to hours of the day and seasons, just as a painter's palette shifts from dawn to night. Play any of them.

Light is one octave

Visible light runs from about 400 THz (deep red) to about 790 THz (violet), almost exactly a doubling of frequency, which in music is an octave. Newton divided the spectrum into seven colours partly by analogy with the seven notes of a scale.

Kandinsky heard colour

In Concerning the Spiritual in Art (1911) he described yellow as a trumpet, light blue as a flute, dark blue as a cello and violet as a bassoon, and composed paintings like symphonies.

Scriabin's light keyboard

His orchestral work Prometheus (1910) includes a part for a colour organ, the "clavier à lumières", projecting colour in sync with the harmony.

A short history of pigment

From ground earth to laboratory accidents: every new pigment changed what painters could do.

c. 2600 BCEEgyptian blue: one of the first synthetic pigments, made by heating sand, copper and lime.
c. 1500 BCETyrian purple from murex sea snails in the Phoenician cities.
6th–7th c.Ultramarine from Afghan lapis lazuli appears in Buddhist cave paintings at Bamiyan.

Which way do your windows face?

The same paint looks different in every room because daylight changes with direction. These notes are for the northern hemisphere; swap north and south below the equator.

North-facing

Cool, even, indirect light all day. Colours read cooler and greyer. Warm undertones (yellow or red-based neutrals) balance it, or embrace depth with a rich dark colour.

East-facing

Warm, bright light in the morning, cooler and dimmer by afternoon. Choose for when you use the room; soft warm colours keep it pleasant later in the day.

South-facing

Warm, strong light most of the day. Cool colours stay fresh here and warm colours can tip into hot; most colours look their best.

West-facing

Cooler, dim light in the morning, warm golden light in the evening. Colours glow at sunset; test in the afternoon too.

How to test a colour

  1. Narrow down to three colours, not ten.
  2. Paint large samples, at least 50 × 50 cm (20 × 20 in), with two coats, or use painted sample boards.
  3. Put them on more than one wall, including the darkest and the brightest.
  4. Look in the morning, at midday, in the evening and under your own bulbs, over two or three days.
  5. Hold a sheet of white paper next to each sample to see its undertone.

Wall surfaces

Drywall (plasterboard)

Smooth and uniform; colour reads evenly. New board needs a primer so the joints do not show through.

Smooth plaster

Faint movement in the surface, softer than drywall. Fresh plaster needs a thinned mist coat once fully dry.

Limewash or mineral paint

Chalky, mottled and breathable; the colour changes with the light and from wall to wall. For lime plaster and masonry.

Brick

Texture and mortar lines break the colour up and make it read darker. Use a breathable masonry paint.

Wood panelling

Tongue-and-groove or wainscot: shadow lines add rhythm. Seal knots with a stain-blocking primer.

Concrete or microcement

Cool and cloudy. Usually left raw and sealed, or tinted.

Tile

Glossy and grid-lined; grout colour changes the overall tone a lot.

Types of paint

Water-based acrylic or latex

The everyday wall paint: low odour, quick drying, wide colour choice.

Waterborne enamel

Hard, washable trim and cabinet paint without solvent fumes.

Alkyd or oil-based

Very hard and smooth, but slow-drying, solvent-heavy, and whites yellow in the dark.

Mineral (silicate)

Bonds into masonry and plaster; very breathable, very matte.

Lime paint and limewash

Natural, breathable, mottled; suits old buildings and lime plaster.

Clay paint

Deeply matte, soft and breathable; good for bedrooms.

Chalk paint

For furniture: sticks to most surfaces with little sanding; needs wax or varnish.

Choose low- or zero-VOC paints where you can, keep windows open while painting and drying, and keep children and pets out of fresh rooms.

Light reflectance value (LRV)

LRV says how much light a colour reflects, from 0 for black to 100 for a perfect white. Most whites sit between 80 and 90, mid-tones between 30 and 60, and deep colours under 15. Below about 50, a room needs more light to feel bright. The room studio shows the LRV of every wall colour you try.

Light changes every colour.

Bulb colour, colour rendering and brightness, room by room, and what each does to your walls, your art and your skin.

Colour temperature

Measured in kelvin (K): low numbers are warm and amber, high numbers cool and blue. Each square shows the wall colour you choose here as it looks under that light, once your eyes have partly adjusted.

2200KCandlelight

Amber glow for evenings, dining and bedside lamps. Flattens blues and greens.

2700KWarm white

Like an old incandescent bulb. Living rooms, bedrooms, dining. Whites read creamy.

3000KSoft white

Halogen-like. Kitchens, bathrooms and living spaces that should feel warm but clear.

3500KNeutral warm

Offices and kitchens: a balance between cosy and crisp.

4000KNeutral white

Workspaces, utility rooms and task lighting. Clean colour, can feel clinical at night.

5000KDaylight

Studios and colour-critical work (with CRI 95+). Matches midday sun; harsh at home in the evening.

6500KCool daylight

Overcast-sky blue-white. Screens and inspection; rarely right in a home.

Colour rendering (CRI)

CRI (Ra) says how faithfully a light shows colours compared with a reference light of the same colour temperature, on a scale to 100. 80 is basic; 90 or more is good for homes; 95 or more for painting and colour matching. Also check R9, the score for saturated red: it governs skin, wood and food. A low-CRI bulb can make a carefully chosen wall look dull or muddy whatever its colour temperature.

Daylight and the body clock

Bright, cooler light in the morning and day and warmer, dimmer light in the evening follows the natural daylight cycle and helps you wind down at night. Tunable or warm-dim bulbs make this easy.

Types of bulb

LED

Efficient and long-lived. Choose CRI 90+ (R9 above 50 for good reds and skin), flicker-free, and dimmable if your switch is a dimmer.

Warm-dim LED

Gets warmer as it dims, from about 2700K to 1800–2200K, like an incandescent bulb. Lovely for dining rooms and bedrooms.

Tunable white

Smart bulbs that change colour temperature: cooler and brighter in the day, warmer and dimmer in the evening.

Filament LED

Decorative clear bulbs with a visible filament. Usually warm (2200–2700K); check the CRI.

Halogen

Excellent colour (CRI near 100) but inefficient and hot; phased out for general use in many countries.

Fluorescent / CFL

Often lower CRI and a slight green cast; contains mercury. Being replaced by LED.
